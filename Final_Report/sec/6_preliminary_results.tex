\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Unless stated otherwise, predictive results come from the single locked final test (545 students,
213 dropouts), whereas the explanation-audit and sensitivity results use validation data only and
are post-hoc.

\subsection{Predictive Performance on the Locked Final Test}
\label{sec:perf}

Table~\ref{tab:final-test} and Figure~\ref{fig:final-test} summarise the five models. Apart from the
depth-3 tree, the models were almost indistinguishable: ROC-AUC ranged from 0.953 for logistic
regression to 0.957 for \tabicl, a spread of only 0.004, and at the fixed capacity all five flagged
between 107 and 109 of the 213 dropouts. XGBoost achieved the best Brier score and calibration,
while EBM achieved the best capacity recall and precision, as all 109 students it flagged dropped
out. \tabicl flagged 109 students, of whom 108 dropped out (precision 0.991, recall 0.507;
TN\,=\,331, FP\,=\,1, FN\,=\,105, TP\,=\,108).

\subsection{Why Precision Is Nearly Perfect: Who Gets Flagged}
\label{sec:who}

A precision of 0.99 to 1.00 warrants scrutiny rather than celebration. On the validation split,
\tabicl flagged 108 students, all of whom dropped out, and Table~\ref{tab:who} characterises them:
78 passed no first-semester units and 25 took no evaluations at all, while 19 of the 30 who passed
at least one unit had unpaid tuition fees. A one-line rule, ``flag students who passed zero
units'', selects 103 students with a precision of 0.874 and a recall of 0.423, compared with 1.000
and 0.507 for the model. The model's added value is accordingly narrow: it excludes 25 zero-pass
students (13 of whom graduated) and adds 30 students with some passed units, most of whom had fee
problems. For most flagged students, an adviser would learn little beyond what the transcript
already shows, and the flags arrive when these students may already have disengaged.

\subsection{Calibration and Subgroup Diagnostics}
\label{sec:fairness}

Calibration error was small for all models except logistic regression and the decision tree
(Table~\ref{tab:final-test}). Subgroup results on the final test are descriptive, and metrics are
suppressed for groups of fewer than 30 students (all non-Portuguese nationalities, international
students and students with special educational needs). Flag rates differed by gender (0.122 for
group~0, female in the UCI coding, vs.\ 0.324 for group~1, male), displacement (0.136 for displaced
vs.\ 0.288 for non-displaced students) and age (0.116 for students aged 20 or younger vs.\ 0.434 for
ages 26--35). These differences largely follow the dropout base rates (0.30 vs.\ 0.54 by gender),
but recall also differed, at 0.410 vs.\ 0.593 by gender, 0.414 vs.\ 0.596 by displacement and
0.411 vs.\ 0.655 between the youngest and the 26--35 age bands. The model thus identifies a smaller
share of dropouts among younger, female and displaced students (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:subgroups}).
In addition, several non-protected features act as strong proxies for protected attributes:
application route is associated with international status (Cram\'er's $V=0.61$) and age (0.50), and
course with gender (0.40). The model's own leading inputs carry the same signal more weakly: on the
final test, first-semester pass rate is associated with gender ($\eta=0.28$) and age (0.24), and fee
status with age ($V=0.23$), while parental and financial fields may encode socio-economic
disadvantage. Removing protected columns, in short, cannot remove their information.

\subsection{Explanation Faithfulness and Stability}
\label{sec:audit}

\textbf{Faithfulness.} Removing groups in SHAP order changed the prediction far more than removing
them in random order: the mean area between the curves was 1.557 (95\% interval 1.476--1.643) and was
positive for 536 of 544 students (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:faithfulness}). The explanations thus
point to information that the model actually uses.

\textbf{Content of the explanations.} First-semester progress dominated (mean absolute grouped SHAP
0.277, in the top five for 97\% of students), followed by participation (0.053), tuition fees
(0.051) and course (0.042) (Figure~\ref{fig:top-groups}). As first-semester progress was the
leading reason for every flagged student, the explanation largely restates the transcript for
precisely the students who matter most. Figure~\ref{fig:card} illustrates how such an explanation
could reach a support officer: a flagged validation student who sat ten evaluations but passed no
unit, whose progress bar outweighs every other reason.

\textbf{Global explanations.} Three global views, computed independently of the local SHAP values,
agree with this ranking. Grouped permutation importance on 100 validation students (5 repeats)
places first-semester progress far ahead (ROC-AUC loss 0.255), followed by tuition fees (0.038) and
participation (0.025), while every other group costs less than 0.005. The separately fitted EBM
gives its largest mean contributions to first-semester pass rate, tuition fees, course,
first-semester grade and scholarship status. Finally, ALE curves for admission grade, age at
enrolment and credited units stay within $\pm0.03$ of the average prediction, so these inputs move
the estimate little once first-semester results are known. This agreement across methods supports
the view that the local explanations reflect the model's actual reliance on first-semester
progress rather than an artefact of SHAP.

\textbf{Stability.} The ranking of reasons was robust to minimal grade changes (mean top-five
overlap 0.996; no student below 0.80) but considerably less robust to the explainer's own settings: overlap was 0.818
across random seeds (213 students below 0.80) and 0.745 across background samples (411 students
below 0.80) (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}).

\textbf{Recourse.} Only 17 of the 108 flagged students would leave the top 20\% if their debt or
fee status changed; for the remaining 91, no permitted change removes the flag. For validation
student 174, for example, recording the fees as paid and the debt as cleared lowers the estimate
from 0.996 to 0.689 and the cohort rank from 87 to 182, outside the 108 slots
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-interface}).

\textbf{Additivity and interactions.} The bar chart presents each reason as a separate effect, an
assumption that was tested directly. Masking pairs of each student's top five groups jointly rather
than separately revealed a mean absolute non-additive response of 0.036 probability units, the
largest common pair being first-semester progress with participation (0.197 across 320 students).
The best local surrogate (lasso with pair terms) approximated \tabicl with a mean absolute error of
0.022 on held-out masking patterns, against 0.073 without pair terms, and pair-term surrogates were
more accurate for 95\% of students. The model's local behaviour is therefore captured well only once
pairwise interactions are included, and the five bars, read as independent effects, understate how
the two first-semester groups act together.

\textbf{Summing the bars.} SHAP contributions sum by construction to the prediction minus the
comparison-group average, which means that all grouped contributions together reproduce the ranking
exactly. Even the five bars shown to participants separate flagged from unflagged students (AUC
0.97; 89 of 108 flags recovered).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analyses}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\textbf{Enrolled students.} The frozen model scores still-enrolled students between the two known
groups (median 0.354, compared with 0.064 for graduates and 0.990 for dropouts). When 119 enrolled
students, a share proportional to the validation split, are added to the cohort, they receive 7 of
the 132 flags (5\%) despite forming 18\% of the cohort; with the number of slots held at 108, three flags move to enrolled students and
dropout recall falls from 0.507 to 0.493. Whether these seven flags are correct remains unknown.

\textbf{Eligibility.} When the 49 validation students who took no first-semester evaluation (36 of
them dropouts) were set aside and the remaining 495 re-ranked with the frozen model, the top 20\%
(99 students) still contained only dropouts (precision 1.000), recall rose to 0.559 and ROC-AUC was
0.949 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). The flag is thus not merely detecting students who had
already stopped attending, although the 99 flagged students remain those with weak first-semester
results.

\textbf{Admission-time window.} Without first-semester results, the validation ROC-AUC of \tabicl
fell from 0.952 to 0.874 and capacity recall from 0.507 to 0.469 (precision 0.926), with similar
declines for all models, which confirms that first-semester results carry most of the signal.

\textbf{Without protected attributes.} Refitting \tabicl without gender, nationality, age,
international status, displacement and special needs changed the validation ROC-AUC from 0.952 to
0.950, left recall and precision at 0.507 and 1.000, and retained 103 of the 108 flags (the five
students swapped in and the five swapped out were all dropouts). The gender recall gap narrowed from
0.19 (0.404 vs.\ 0.597) to 0.10 (0.455 vs.\ 0.553).

\subsection{Human-Study Quantitative Results}
\label{sec:human}

All 50 assigned participants consented and completed the study, and none were excluded, which
yielded 500 scored responses. Participants described themselves as students (23), staff or advisers
(23, a broad option) or other (4); students and staff or advisers reached similar accuracy. Mean accuracy was 0.916 with
profiles only and 0.900 with explanation charts (Table~\ref{tab:human}), a difference of $-0.016$
(95\% interval $-0.060$ to 0.032; exploratory permutation $p=0.61$; Hedges' $g=-0.19$). There is
thus no evidence that the charts helped participants predict the flag. Participants with charts
were, however, slightly more confident ($+0.21$ points on a 1--5 scale, 0.02 to 0.42; $p=0.055$),
while self-reported completion time showed no clear difference.

Accuracy was close to the ceiling in both groups: in eight of the ten cases, at least 92\% of each
group answered correctly, as flagged profiles usually showed no passed units. This ceiling left
little room for the charts to improve accuracy. With charts, accuracy rose on flagged cases (0.968 to
0.992) and fell on unflagged ones (0.864 to 0.808), the fall coming entirely from one case. Nine of
the ten cases had equal or higher accuracy with charts. The exception, case C01, was not flagged,
yet its chart showed several factors pushing risk upwards, and accuracy fell from 0.60 without the
chart to 0.20 with it (Figure~\ref{fig:human-case}). As the chart shows contributions relative to a
typical student rather than to the cohort cut-off, it invites the inference that ``more concern
means flagged''. Without C01, the difference becomes $+0.027$ ($-0.013$ to 0.076); this post-hoc
check shows that the overall result depends on a single case but does not establish a benefit. In
Group~B, 21 of 25 participants passed both chart-reading checks.

\subsection{Qualitative Findings}
\label{sec:qual}

The written answers are summarised from a single coding pass (Section~\ref{sec:coding}). When asked
why the model decided as it did, 72\% of chart-group answers reasoned from the chart, whereas the
profile-only group cited academic results (46\%) or several factors together (46\%); personal or
demographic reasons were almost never given (2 of 500). Suggested support was mostly academic
(30\%), followed by no action (21\%, mostly for unflagged students), advising or monitoring (16\%),
financial or administrative help (15\%) and first establishing what had happened (11\%), while
wellbeing support was rare (2\%). Overall, 76\% of suggestions named a concrete next step (71\%
without charts and 82\% with them), 91\% could begin before the second semester and 2.8\% asserted an
unsupported cause; the chart group also assigned the action to the institution more often (42\% vs.\
27\%). Asked what else they would want to know, participants most often named academic engagement,
such as attendance and current enrolment (40\%), and personal circumstances, such as work or family
(26\%), none of which the dataset contains. Typical answers, paraphrased, were that the long
first-semester bars pushed the estimate towards a flag; that the office should contact the student
about unpaid fees, offer a payment plan and then arrange tutoring; and that it would help to know
whether the student still attends and how many hours they work. These are written comments rather
than think-aloud remarks, given the online format. Answer depth also varied: some answers were
generic, and 21 support suggestions matched another participant's answer word for word.
